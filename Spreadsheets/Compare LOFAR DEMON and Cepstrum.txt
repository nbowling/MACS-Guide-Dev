1. LOFAR

Think of LOFAR as your spectral map.

Starting with acoustic data:

x(t)x(t)

you perform spectral analysis such as an FFT:

x(t)→X(f)x(t)\rightarrow X(f)

A sequence of spectra displayed against time gives you the familiar LOFARgram.

What you look for

For example, suppose the spectrum contains:

29.7,31.0,32.3,33.6 Hz.29.7,\quad 31.0,\quad32.3,\quad33.6\text{ Hz}.

LOFAR tells you:

These spectral structures exist.

You can then investigate:

individual tonals
harmonic relationships
sidebands
persistent versus transient features
spectral changes over time

But the presence of a tonal is not automatically proof of a particular machinery source, a caution specifically made in your review.

2. DEMON

DEMON asks a quite different question:

Does the received acoustic energy contain periodic amplitude modulation?

Conceptually:

Acoustic signal
      │
      ▼
Select appropriate band
      │
      ▼
Envelope / demodulation
      │
      ▼
Analyse envelope spectrum
      │
      ▼
Modulation frequencies


So if DEMON shows a strong peak at:

11.7 Hz,11.7\text{ Hz},

the careful interpretation is:

There is significant periodic modulation at approximately 11.7 Hz.

It isn't automatically:

“There is an acoustic tonal at 11.7 Hz.”

And it isn't automatically:

“The propeller blade rate is 11.7 Hz.”

You subsequently compare that modulation frequency with the physical hypotheses.

For example, if independently established shaft rate is SRSR and the propeller has BB blades:

BR=B×SR.BR=B\times SR.

Your review specifically recommends exactly this kind of physics-based cross-checking.

3. Cepstrum

Cepstrum asks something different again:

Is there periodic spacing among features in the spectrum?

The simplified processing chain we've just explored is:

x(t)x(t)

↓

Fourier transform

X(f)X(f)

↓

Magnitude

∣X(f)∣|X(f)|

↓

Log magnitude

ln⁡∣X(f)∣\ln |X(f)|

↓

Inverse transform

c(q)c(q)

The result is the cepstrum, whose horizontal coordinate is quefrency.

The important relationship is:

Δfspacing≈1q\boxed{ \Delta f_{\mathrm{spacing}}\approx\frac{1}{q} }

with qq expressed in seconds.

4. A combined example

Imagine LOFAR shows a family:

50.0,51.3,52.6,53.9,55.2 Hz.50.0,\quad51.3,\quad52.6,\quad53.9,\quad55.2\text{ Hz}.

The spacing appears to be:

1.3 Hz.\boxed{1.3\text{ Hz}}.
LOFAR tells us

There is a family of persistent spectral features with approximately 1.3 Hz separation.

Cepstrum tests that relationship

The predicted quefrency is:

q=11.3≈0.769 s.q=\frac{1}{1.3} \approx0.769\text{ s}.

So a cepstral feature around:

769 ms\boxed{769\text{ ms}}

would independently support the existence of approximately 1.3 Hz periodic spectral spacing.

DEMON asks something else

DEMON might then reveal periodic modulation at 1.3 Hz, 6.5 Hz or another physically meaningful rate.

That gives us a different piece of evidence:

The received acoustic energy is being periodically modulated at that rate.

This is considerably stronger than simply saying “There's a line at 51.3 Hz, therefore we've identified the machinery.”

5. Their outputs should not be confused

This is probably the most important comparison.

Suppose you see:

LOFAR peak at 8 Hz

It means:

spectral energy/tonal structure near 8 Hz\boxed{\text{spectral energy/tonal structure near 8 Hz}}
DEMON peak at 8 Hz

It means:

periodic modulation near 8 Hz\boxed{\text{periodic modulation near 8 Hz}}
Cepstral evidence corresponding to 8 Hz

The quefrency would be:

q=18=0.125 s=125 ms.q=\frac18=0.125\text{ s}=125\text{ ms}.

That means:

spectral features recur at roughly 8 Hz intervals\boxed{\text{spectral features recur at roughly 8 Hz intervals}}

Those are three different observations.

They could be related to the same underlying machinery, but the processing itself doesn't establish that.
