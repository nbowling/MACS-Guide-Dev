12. The intimidating formula

Column H contains the big DFT magnitude formula.

Conceptually, it's doing this:

=scale *
 SQRT(
   SUMPRODUCT(signal,COS(reference))^2
   +
   SUMPRODUCT(signal,SIN(reference))^2
 )


That looks terrible when written as one enormous Excel formula.

But there are really only four operations:

Generate cosine and sine reference patterns.
Multiply them by the measured signal and sum.
Combine the two resulting values.
Scale the answer.

Let's take them one at a time.

13. The cosine calculation

The first major component is effectively:

SUMPRODUCT(
 $E$11:$E$1010,
 COS(2*PI()*F11*$A$11:$A$1010/$B$4)
)

R[k]=∑n=0N−1x[n]cos⁡(2πknN).R[k]= \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n] \cos\left(\frac{2\pi kn}{N}\right).
What does this actually mean?

Suppose Excel is calculating bin:

k=50.k=50.

It creates a 50 Hz cosine reference pattern.

It then does:

x[0]×cos⁡(θ0)x[0]\times\cos(\theta_0)

plus:

x[1]×cos⁡(θ1)x[1]\times\cos(\theta_1)

plus:

x[2]×cos⁡(θ2)x[2]\times\cos(\theta_2)

and continues all the way to:

x[999]×cos⁡(θ999).x[999]\times\cos(\theta_{999}).

SUMPRODUCT conveniently performs all 1,000 multiplications and additions for us.

15. Why does this detect a frequency?

Suppose we're testing:

49 Hz.49\text{ Hz}.

The actual signal contains 50 and 120 Hz.

The 49 Hz reference does not consistently line up with either.

Across the one-second observation interval, positive and negative products mostly cancel.

So:

R[49]≈0.R[49]\approx0.

Now test:

50 Hz.50\text{ Hz}.

The 50 Hz reference aligns with the 50 Hz component in our measured signal.

The products now reinforce one another.

So:

R[50]R[50]

becomes significant.

This is the heart of Fourier analysis.

16. The sine calculation

The formula performs a second SUMPRODUCT:

SUMPRODUCT(
 $E$11:$E$1010,
 SIN(2*PI()*F11*$A$11:$A$1010/$B$4)
)


Mathematically:

I[k]=∑n=0N−1x[n]sin⁡(2πknN).I[k]= \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n] \sin\left(\frac{2\pi kn}{N}\right).

It performs the same sort of comparison, but against a sine reference.

Why both?

Because our unknown frequency component may have an arbitrary phase.

A pure cosine and a pure sine at the same frequency are 90° apart in phase.

By examining both, the DFT can detect the frequency regardless of that initial phase.

19. Why we need normalisation

The raw DFT magnitude depends upon NN.

A bin-centred sinusoid with amplitude AA has, for the ordinary positive-frequency bin, a raw DFT magnitude of approximately:

NA2.\frac{NA}{2}.

Our 50 Hz sinusoid has:

A=3A=3

and:

N=1000.N=1000.

Therefore its raw magnitude is approximately:

1000×32=1500.\frac{1000\times3}{2} =1500.

But that's not a very intuitive answer.

We know we generated a sinusoid with amplitude:

3.3.

So the workbook applies:

2N\boxed{\frac{2}{N}}

to ordinary positive-frequency bins.

Therefore:

1500×21000=3.1500\times\frac{2}{1000} =3.

Our Fourier spectrum now reports an amplitude of:

3\boxed{3}

at 50 Hz.

Likewise the 120 Hz component becomes:

2.\boxed{2}.

The key insight

If you remember only one thing about the formulas, make it this:

For each candidate frequency, the DFT generates sine and cosine references at that frequency, compares every signal sample against them, sums the results, and combines those sums into a magnitude.

So the spectrum is effectively the result of asking:

0 Hz, how much of you is present?
 1 Hz, how much of you is present?
 2 Hz, how much of you is present?
 ⋮
 50 Hz? Lots.
 ⋮
 120 Hz? Lots.
 ⋮

That is why those two peaks emerge even though the original time-domain waveform looks considerably more complicated.

And once this mechanism is clear, FFT becomes much less mysterious: fundamentally it obtains the same DFT result, but uses a vastly more efficient computational algorithm to get there.

1. The DFT equation

The mathematical DFT is:

X[k]=∑n=0N−1x[n]e−j2πkn/N\boxed{ X[k]= \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n]e^{-j2\pi kn/N} }

That looks much worse than what it actually means.

The most important idea is:

For one output frequency bin kk, take every sample x[n]x[n], multiply it by an appropriate sine/cosine value, and add all those results together.

Then repeat the process for the next frequency bin.

So the DFT is essentially a large collection of tests:

Is 0 Hz present?
 Is 1 Hz present?
 Is 2 Hz present?
 ...
 Is 35 Hz present?
 ...
 Is 80 Hz present?

2. What nn and kk mean

This distinction is crucial.

nn means sample number
n=0,1,2,…,N−1n=0,1,2,\ldots,N-1

It moves through the time-domain samples.

kk means frequency-bin number
k=0,1,2,…,N−1k=0,1,2,\ldots,N-1

It moves through the frequency-domain bins.

So:

n=input sample\boxed{n=\text{input sample}}

while:

k=output frequency bin\boxed{k=\text{output frequency bin}}

Don't let the fact that both are integers confuse you. They have completely different jobs.

13. Why do we need both sine and cosine?

Because an unknown sinusoid may have an arbitrary phase.

Suppose we only tested against a sine.

A signal:

would correlate strongly.

But a 90° shifted version behaves like:

Acos⁡(2πft).A\cos(2\pi ft).

Testing with both sine and cosine means the DFT can detect the frequency regardless of its phase.

This is extremely important.
